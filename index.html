Luxury Relevance Index
Live app ↗GitHub ↗
Is luxury losing its appeal, or does it just look that way in the data?
What I found
  • Most of the apparent decline isn’t about luxury. Wikipedia views of luxury brands fell, but views of unrelated articles fell by a similar amount, so a large part of the drop was site-wide.
  • Brands moved very differently. Hermès held steady across both search and Wikipedia, while Gucci’s search interest fell by about 40% between 2019 and 2025.
  • “Quiet luxury” became a lasting term, with a clear jump in April 2023.
  • Attention couldn’t predict itself. Searches and Wikipedia views did not reliably lead each other, so I could not build an early-warning signal.
Why it mattersReading a fall in a raw attention chart as “declining interest” would mislead a brand team, so the first step is to compare against a control. And because Hermès held steady while Gucci fell, the causes look brand-specific rather than category-wide. Creative direction, pricing and campaigns are the hypotheses I would test next, using sales data and company reports. Attention is also not spending, so these results show interest, not revenue.
How I did it
  • Compared Google Trends searches (3 brands, 4 luxury terms) with Wikipedia page views from 2019 to 2026, using unrelated articles as a baseline.
  • Applied STL decomposition, PELT change-point detection, correlations, Granger tests and walk-forward forecasting in Python.
  • Stored the time series in SQLite and queried it with SQL joins, CTEs and window functions.
  • Built a Streamlit dashboard with a relevance index.
Technologies used -PythonPandasGoogle TrendsTime seriesStreamlit
Bike Sharing Demand
GitHub ↗
What drives bike rental demand, and can it be predicted well enough to plan around?
What I found
  • Demand follows the seasons. Rentals peak from June to September.
  • The business was growing. Rentals were clearly higher in 2019 than in 2018.
  • The model predicts well. On days it had never seen, it was off by about 615 rentals a day on average (R² of 0.83).
Why it mattersAn operator could use this to schedule bike supply and maintenance around the summer peak and to plan for growth, with a rough daily forecast to back it up. The data covers one system over two years, so I would not trust it for a different city without retesting.
How I did it
  • Analyzed 730 days of weather, season and calendar data (2018–2019).
  • Removed leakage columns (casual, registered) and a redundant temperature column so the model could not “cheat.”
  • One-hot encoded categorical variables and visualized demand with Matplotlib and Seaborn.
  • Built a Linear Regression model. Train R² was 0.85, so overfitting was minimal.
Technologies used -PandasNumPyMatplotlibSeabornScikit-learn
Heart Disease Risk
GitHub ↗
Can a few clinical measurements flag which patients are at risk of heart disease?
What I found
  • Yes, reasonably well. The best model (KNN) correctly identified 94% of patients who had heart disease, with 84% accuracy overall.
  • It beat three other approaches (Logistic Regression, SVM, XGBoost) after tuning them all with GridSearchCV.
Why it mattersFor screening, catching sick patients matters more than avoiding false alarms, which is why I focused on recall. This is a learning project on 303 records from one dataset, so it is not clinical evidence. It shows how I choose the metric that fits the real-world cost of a mistake.
How I did it
  • Worked with 303 patient records and 13 clinical features.
  • Checked missing values, one-hot encoded categoricals, standardized continuous features and used a stratified 70/30 split.
  • Explored distributions and a correlation heatmap to find the strongest relationships.
  • Evaluated with precision, recall, F1 and confusion matrices.
Technologies used -PandasSeabornScikit-learnXGBoostGridSearchCV
